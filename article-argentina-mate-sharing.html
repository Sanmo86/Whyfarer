The Straw That Passes From Mouth to Mouth, and Nobody Minds

Sharing a single mate straw among strangers would break just about every Western hygiene norm. In Argentina, Uruguay, and Paraguay, it's exactly the opposite: the clearest gesture of trust and community there is.

WHYFARER Editors6 min read🇦🇷 Argentina

What's Really Going On

In a park in Buenos Aires, a group of friends sits on the grass, a thermos of hot water at the center, and a mate — the small gourd-like vessel, traditionally made from dried squash — passing from hand to hand. Each person drinks straight from the same metal straw, the same tube already used by the four or five people before them, with no wiping, no hesitation, no visible discomfort whatsoever. To someone from a culture where sharing a glass with a stranger already raises eyebrows, the scene can feel almost transgressive. And yet, in Argentina, Uruguay, and much of Paraguay, it's exactly the opposite: a social ritual loaded with meaning and closeness.

Mate, brewed from the dried, crushed leaves of the yerba mate plant, is far more than a caffeinated drink in this part of the world — it's a structured social ritual with its own unwritten rules, passed down generation after generation almost without ever needing to be spelled out. There's always one person in charge of "serving" — preparing and pouring the mate for each round, usually whoever organized the gathering or hosts the home — and that same person drinks first from each fresh brew, not out of selfishness, but because the first sips tend to come out more bitter or unbalanced, sparing guests from them.

Sharing a single straw among the whole group, far from being seen as a hygiene issue, is culturally understood as the very heart of the ritual: it's precisely that gesture of shared physical intimacy — literally drinking from the same spot already touched by the lips of your friends, family, or even people you just met — that turns mate into a symbol of community, trust, and belonging. Declining an invitation to drink mate, with no clear reason (like disliking its bitter taste), can be read as a small social distance, almost like turning down a hug or a gesture of closeness offered in good faith.

Turning down a mate someone offers you, or drinking it the wrong way, can come across as a small social snub in Argentina.

Where This Comes From

This ritual traces back to the Guaraní peoples, native to the region that today spans Argentina, Paraguay, Uruguay, and southern Brazil, who were already drinking yerba mate for social, medicinal, and even ceremonial purposes long before Spanish colonizers arrived. Over time the custom spread and evolved, but kept its collective essence: mate is almost never prepared or drunk alone as its main purpose — though plenty of people do drink it solo as part of their daily routine — but reaches its deepest meaning when shared in a circle, whether at an office, a park, a family home, or during a game of soccer among friends.

Mate also has its own silent code of communication that anyone from the region absorbs almost without realizing it, from childhood: the straw is always positioned the same way for whoever's receiving the mate, it's never stirred or moved around while drinking — that would ruin the brew for the next person's turn — and to signal you don't want more, a simple "thanks" when handing it back functions, in this specific context, almost like an official exit signal from the round, while handing it back in silence simply means "yes, I'll have another."

✦

Fun fact. Mate has its own silent code: if the person serving hands it to you with the straw pointed your way, it's your turn; handing it back with a simple spoken "thanks," on the other hand, means you're done and officially opting out of the round.

Beyond the Basics

In recent years, partly for public-health reasons tied to the spread of viruses like the common cold or oral infections, some people have started bringing their own personal straw to group gatherings — a modern adaptation that coexists without much friction alongside the tradition of shared mate. The covid-19 pandemic, in particular, sparked a real public debate across the region about the custom, though for most of the population the ritual survived largely intact once the most acute phase of the health emergency passed.

While the ritual is most associated with Argentina, mate is just as central to daily life in Uruguay — which actually has the highest per-capita mate consumption in the world — and in Paraguay, where a cold version called tereré is preferred in the country's punishing summer heat. Southern Brazil, particularly the state of Rio Grande do Sul, shares the same gaucho-rooted mate culture, proof that the ritual belongs to a shared regional identity that predates the modern national borders drawn across it.

Mate has also gone mobile: it's now common to see Argentines walking down the street balancing a mate gourd in one hand and a thermos tucked under the other arm, refilling on the go between errands. Global visibility got a boost from athletes like footballer Lionel Messi and rugby players routinely sipping mate on camera during international tournaments, turning a very local ritual into something millions of foreign fans now recognize on sight, even if they've never tasted it.

What to Know If You Visit

If someone hands you a mate, here's how to do it right:

  • If offered mate, drink the whole thing before handing it back — leaving liquid is considered impolite.
  • Don't stir the bombilla (straw) or move it once placed — it disrupts the brew for the next person.
  • Bringing your own bombilla to a group gathering is increasingly common and totally acceptable.
  • Saying "gracias" when you hand the mate back signals you're done for the round — stay silent if you want more.
  • It's fine to politely decline mate altogether at the start — just say so clearly, rather than accepting and leaving it undrunk.

The Bigger Picture

In the end, what makes mate unique isn't so much the drink itself — bitter, caffeinated, with an intense herbal taste — but the social structure built around it: a ritual that literally forces a pause, sitting down with other people, and sharing, in the most physical sense possible, the same object and the same moment. In a region where time spent with friends and family is deeply valued, the straw passed from mouth to mouth isn't a hygiene oversight: it's, perhaps, one of the most honest symbols of what it means to trust the people close to you.
