The Houses With No Curtains That Show Off Their Private Life

Walking a Dutch street at night feels like touring a gallery of lit-up living rooms. No one hides them, and that says more than it seems about Dutch society.

WHYFARER Editors6 min read🇳🇱 The Netherlands

Walking through any Dutch residential neighborhood at nightfall is a strange experience if you're not used to it: house windows are left completely uncovered, lit up, and from the street you can see the living room with total clarity — the dinner table, sometimes even dinner being served or a family watching TV. No curtains drawn, no blinds down, none of the instinct to hide the domestic interior that feels almost automatic in so many other cultures. To a first-time visitor, it feels like accidentally touring an exhibition of private lives voluntarily put on display for the public.

The most commonly cited — and probably deepest — explanation has religious roots going back to Calvinism, the Protestant movement that shaped much of Dutch identity from the 16th century onward. Calvinism emphasized moral transparency and honest living as core values: having nothing to hide, not even inside your own home, became a way of publicly demonstrating integrity. Closing your curtains, under that historical logic, could be read as a sign that there was something shameful or dishonest to conceal, while keeping the house visible communicated an orderly life with no secrets.

Alongside this religious root sits an equally important urban and practical factor: the Netherlands is one of Europe's most densely populated countries, with narrow streets, houses packed tightly together, and enormous windows — a historic architectural feature originally designed to maximize natural light in a country that's gray and rainy much of the year. In that context, permanently closing curtains would turn every house into a dark box for much of the day, something many Dutch people simply consider impractical, even depressing, given the natural lack of sunlight.

In the Netherlands, keeping your curtains closed during the day can be read as a sign that you have something to hide.

There's also a contemporary social explanation beyond religion: showing off the inside of your home is seen as a way of visually participating in the neighborhood community. A house with open curtains signals approachability, normalcy, belonging; a fully sealed-off house, on the other hand, can trigger a certain informal suspicion, almost as if its occupants wanted to deliberately keep themselves apart from the collective life of the street. In many neighborhoods, decorating the living-room window — with plants, ornaments, or even seasonal figures — has become almost a form of public expression, knowing that dozens of neighbors and passersby will see it daily.

This doesn't mean, of course, that the Dutch have no privacy at all: bedrooms and bathrooms do usually have curtains or blinds, and many families close them at night or for specific occasions too. But the general pattern — living-room and kitchen windows fully visible for much of the day and evening — remains surprisingly consistent compared with neighboring countries like Belgium or Germany, where visual privacy at home is valued far more.

✦

Fun fact. Expats even have a nickname for it in English: "Dutch windows" — famous for producing instant astonishment in anyone visiting the country for the first time at night.

For the Dutch themselves, there's also a kind of unwritten code that goes along with this transparency: even though the windows are open, staring into someone else's home while walking down the street is considered rude. The transparency is offered, but not exploited; it's almost a silent pact between neighbors of "I'll show you my life, and you'll discreetly pretend you're not looking too closely." That balance between voluntary exposure and mutual respect is, perhaps, the real key to the phenomenon.

In the end, the Netherlands' famous curtain-less windows are much more than an architectural curiosity for camera-toting tourists: they're the visible reflection of deeply rooted values around honesty, community, and belonging. In a country that spent centuries building a collective identity around the idea of "having nothing to hide," letting a stranger's gaze reach all the way into the living room isn't carelessness — deep down, it's a quiet declaration of trust.
