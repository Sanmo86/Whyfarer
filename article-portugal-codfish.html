The Fish With a Thousand Recipes, and None Repeats All Year

Portugal claims to have a different codfish recipe for every day of the year. The curious part is that the fish isn't even caught off its own coast.

WHYFARER Editors6 min read🇵🇹 Portugal

In any traditional Portuguese restaurant — from a small neighborhood tasca to an elegant spot in downtown Lisbon — you're almost guaranteed to find at least one preparation of bacalhau, salted, cured codfish, on the menu. Over time it became the single most emblematic ingredient in Portuguese national cuisine. There's even a well-worn saying, repeated both inside and outside the country: there are as many codfish recipes as days in the year — three hundred and sixty-five distinct ways to prepare the same fish, never repeating.

What's curious, almost paradoxical, about this culinary obsession is that cod isn't even native to Portuguese waters — it's caught mainly in the North Atlantic, in cold regions near Norway, Iceland, and Newfoundland, far from Portugal's mild Atlantic coast. Portugal's relationship with cod was born, precisely, out of the country's long maritime and exploratory tradition during the Age of Discovery, when Portuguese fishermen began sailing to Nordic waters to catch the fish, salting and drying it on board so it would keep through the long months of the voyage home.

This preservation technique — salting and drying the fish until nearly all its moisture is gone — proved revolutionary for a deeply Catholic nation, where the Church required abstaining from red meat during Lent and on numerous other days throughout the liturgical calendar. Salted cod, which could keep for months without refrigeration — technology that obviously didn't exist yet — became the perfect solution: an accessible, long-lasting protein source, fully compatible with religious restrictions, and available even in inland regions of the country far from the sea.

There's a well-worn Portuguese saying: there are as many codfish recipes as there are days in the year.

Over the centuries, what began as a practical and religious necessity turned into a culinary tradition of its own, deeply woven into Portuguese national identity. Cod went from being a subsistence food tied to scarcity to becoming the centerpiece of major family celebrations, especially Christmas Eve dinner, where the traditional bacalhau cozido com todos — boiled cod with potatoes, cabbage, and egg, drizzled with olive oil — is, for countless Portuguese families, a dish as sacred as any other Christmas symbol.

Among the most celebrated and beloved recipes are bacalhau à Brás, shredded and mixed with thin fried potato straws, scrambled egg, and black olives; bacalhau com natas, oven-baked in a creamy cream sauce; pataniscas de bacalhau, crispy fried fritters served as a starter or main dish alongside tomato rice; and bacalhau à Gomes de Sá, layered with potato, caramelized onion, hard-boiled egg, and olives, originally from the city of Porto. Every region, every family, and even every restaurant tends to claim they make "the real" version of one of these dishes, sparking passionate culinary debates among the Portuguese.

✦

Fun fact. Portugal, with barely 10 million people, has historically been one of the world's largest consumers and importers of salted cod, even though the fish lives in the cold waters of the North Atlantic, far from its coast.

Preparing cod properly also requires a careful desalting process that can take twenty-four to forty-eight hours, changing the water several times to remove excess salt without losing the cured fish's characteristic texture. This need for advance planning further reinforces its status as a special-occasion dish, made with time and care rather than thrown together on a weeknight — which also feeds into the emotional, festive weight surrounding every preparation.

Today, most of the cod eaten in Portugal is still imported from Norway and Iceland, a trade cycle that has remained largely unchanged for centuries despite advances in fishing and food-preservation technology. That an entire country built such a deep culinary identity around a fish that doesn't even swim in its own waters is, at bottom, a fascinating testament to how history, religion, and practical necessity can turn even the most unlikely ingredient into the very heart of a people's culture.
